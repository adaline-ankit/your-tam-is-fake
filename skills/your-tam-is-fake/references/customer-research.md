# Customer research

Load during demand-evidence work (W2), when designing interview questions, or when the user cites customer enthusiasm as evidence.

## The problem with asking people about your idea

Everyone lies, mostly kindly and unintentionally. Rob Fitzpatrick's framing in *The Mom Test*: the test of a question is whether your mother could answer it wrongly out of love. "Do you think this is a good idea?" fails. "What did you do the last time this happened?" cannot be answered flatteringly, because it is about the past.

**Three rules:**

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The moment you describe the product, the conversation converts to politeness.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not hypotheticals about the future.** Nobody accurately predicts their own purchasing behaviour. Everybody remembers what they actually did.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** If you spoke more than a third of the time, you ran a pitch and collected no data.

## Bad data, and why it feels good

Three categories, all of which register emotionally as validation:

- **Compliments** — "this is really cool," "I love this." Zero information. Fitzpatrick's rule: deflect and re-anchor to their life.
- **Fluff** — generic claims, futures, and hypotheticals. "I would definitely use that." "We're always looking at tools in this space."
- **Feature requests** — useful only when you dig for the *motivation* underneath. A feature request is a customer doing your design job badly; the value is in why they want it.

When a founder brings you "we talked to 30 people and they all loved it," the correct response is to ask what any of those 30 people *did* afterwards. Usually the answer is nothing, and nothing is the finding.

## The only currency that counts: commitment

Compliments are not the currency of a customer conversation — commitment is. Real interest means giving up something scarce. Three currencies:

- **Time** — a scheduled next step with a date, not "let's stay in touch." A follow-up meeting with their colleague present is stronger.
- **Reputation** — an introduction to their boss, their team, or their peers. Referring you costs them social capital, which is why it means something.
- **Money** — a deposit, a pre-order, an LOI, a paid pilot. A *paid* pilot. A free pilot is a time commitment at best.

Every conversation should end in a clear **advance** or a clear **rejection**. A conversation that ends warm and ambiguous produced nothing, and it is the most common outcome. Track advance rate, not conversation count.

Strategist's version of this rule: **when a plan cites customer validation, ask which currency changed hands.** If the answer is none, the validation is enthusiasm, and enthusiasm is free.

## The Four Forces of Progress

Bob Moesta's demand-side model. A switch happens only when the positive forces beat the negative ones. Two positive, two negative — and the negative pair is where most GTM plans have nothing to say.

| Force | What it is | The question |
|---|---|---|
| **F1 Push** | The struggle in their current situation | What happened that made today different from last month? |
| **F2 Pull** | The attraction of the new outcome | What does progress look like to them, in their words? |
| **F3 Habit** | Inertia of the present — sunk cost, familiarity, "it works fine" | What do they lose by changing, including comfort? |
| **F4 Anxiety** | Fear about the new thing — will it work, will I look stupid, who owns it if it breaks | What are they afraid of, and who gets blamed? |

Most GTM plans over-invest in F2 (pull: features, vision, demos) and completely ignore F3 and F4. **Anxiety is usually the binding constraint in B2B**, because the buyer's downside from a failed purchase is personal and the upside is organizational. That asymmetry is why "nobody ever got fired for buying the incumbent" is the strongest competitive moat in enterprise software.

Practical consequence: security certifications, migration tooling, reference customers, pilots, and rollback guarantees are not marketing hygiene — they are **anxiety-reduction mechanisms**, and at enterprise ACV they are frequently the highest-ROI GTM investment available. If the plan has a great pull story and no anxiety-reduction plan, that is a finding.

## The switch interview

Interview people who **recently changed** — bought something, switched vendors, or built an internal tool. Recent switchers can reconstruct the actual timeline; prospects can only speculate.

Reconstruct backwards from the purchase:

1. When did you first realise the old way wasn't working? What happened that day?
2. What did you try first? (There is almost always a free attempt first — a spreadsheet, a script, an intern.)
3. What made you start looking seriously? What was the trigger?
4. Who else got involved, and when? What did each of them need?
5. What nearly stopped the purchase?
6. What did you compare against? (This produces your true competitive set, which is usually not the set in the deck.)
7. How did you justify it internally, and to whom?

The trigger from Q3 is the most commercially valuable sentence in the interview: it is your outbound targeting signal, your campaign timing, and your qualification criterion, all at once.

## Sample sizes that mean something

- **5–8 interviews** in one tight segment: patterns start emerging. Enough to kill an idea, not enough to bet on one.
- **15–20** in one segment: enough to name the trigger and write positioning with confidence.
- **Across segments**: 15 interviews spread over five segments is three per segment, which is anecdote. Depth in one segment beats breadth across five, every time.

## Win/loss analysis

The highest-value research a company with deals can run, and the most commonly skipped.

- **Interview within two weeks** of the decision. Memory decays fast and post-hoc rationalisation sets in.
- **Losses matter more than wins**, and *closed-lost-to-nothing* matters most of all — that is the status quo beating you, which is the real competitor.
- **Require two independent sources** pointing the same way before acting on a pattern. One loss is a story.
- **Route findings to named owners**: positioning → enablement, feature gaps → product, messaging → marketing. Unrouted findings decay into a slide nobody opens.
- **Never let the deal's AE run the interview.** The answers will be about price, because price is the safest thing for a buyer to say and the least threatening thing for a rep to hear.

"We lost on price" is almost always false, and it is the most frequent finding in badly run win/loss programs. Price is the polite reason. Dig for the real one: perceived risk, an unaddressed stakeholder, a missing integration, or a champion who never had budget.
