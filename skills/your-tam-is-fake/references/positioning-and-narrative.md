# Positioning, narrative, and category

Load when writing positioning, auditing messaging, or when someone proposes creating a category.

## Positioning is a decision, not a paragraph

April Dunford's framing: positioning is *deliberately deciding how you are the best at something a specific set of customers cares about.* Most positioning is not decided at all — it is inherited from whatever the founders built first, then defended.

Her diagnostic for bad positioning: the product is being compared against the wrong alternatives, so its real strengths read as irrelevant. A great product positioned in the wrong category loses to a mediocre product positioned in the right one, and it loses without ever understanding why.

## The Dunford sequence

Work it in this order. Order matters more than the content — starting at "market category" is why most positioning is wrong.

**1. Competitive alternatives.** What would the customer do if you did not exist? **Not** the competitors in your deck — the *actual* alternatives, which usually include a spreadsheet, an intern, an agency, and doing nothing.

**2. Unique attributes.** What do you have that the alternatives do not? Features and capabilities, stated factually. No adjectives.

**3. Value.** What can the customer *do* because of those attributes, that they could not otherwise? Value, not features. Each value claim must trace to a specific attribute — an untraceable claim is marketing.

**4. Who cares a lot.** Which customers care disproportionately about that value? Their characteristics *are* your ICP. This is the correct direction of derivation: positioning produces the ICP, not the other way around.

**5. Market category.** What context makes your value obvious to those customers? The category is a *frame that makes your strengths legible*. Choose the frame where your unique attributes are the deciding criteria.

Consequence founders resist: **the best category is often not the biggest one.** A frame where you are obviously best beats a frame that sounds impressive on a slide. "AI platform" is a bigger category and a worse position than "SOC 2 evidence collection for broker-dealers."

## The trap: positioning into the largest market

The pull toward the biggest category is a fundraising instinct, not a GTM one. In the big frame you are compared against well-funded generalists on criteria you do not win. In the narrow frame, buyers arrive already believing you are the specialist.

Test: **write the buyer's shortlist in your chosen category.** If your name looks eccentric next to the others, your positioning is wrong — either the category or the product. Buyers do not stretch frames to accommodate you.

## Strategic narrative

Positioning explains what you are. Narrative explains why the world should change. Andy Raskin's structure, which is the de facto standard in B2B:

1. **Name the shift.** A large, undeniable change in the world — not about you. "Buyers now form shortlists inside AI chat before a vendor knows they exist."
2. **Show the stakes.** Winners and losers on either side of that shift. This is where the enemy is named — and the enemy is *the old way*, never a competitor by name.
3. **Show the promised land.** A future state that is desirable and hard — a state, not a product. If it is easy, nobody needs you.
4. **Introduce the capabilities** as what is required to get there. Your product's features appear here, and only here, framed as the means.
5. **Provide evidence** that people are already crossing. Customers, data, momentum.

Two rules people break constantly: **the customer is the protagonist, not you.** And the promised land must be describable without naming your product — if it cannot be, you have written a brochure.

Narrative is the single highest-leverage artifact in enterprise GTM, because it is what a champion repeats to their CFO when you are not in the room. A pitch survives in a room; a narrative survives being retold badly.

## Category entry vs category creation

Opposite economics. Choose deliberately, and say which you chose.

|  | Category entry | Category creation |
|---|---|---|
| The bet | We win on being better inside an existing frame | We define a new frame and become its default |
| Demand | Exists. Buyers are searching, budget lines exist | Does not exist. You fund the education |
| Cost | Competitive displacement | Market education, measured in years |
| Sales cycle | Shorter — buyer knows the category | Longer — you explain the problem before the product |
| Prize | Market share | Roughly **76%** of category market cap goes to the category king |
| Fails when | You have no real differentiation | You run out of money before the category exists |

**Category creation is a capital strategy disguised as a marketing strategy.** The Play Bigger evidence for the prize is real — the category king takes ~76% of the category's market cap — and it is also survivorship-heavy: the companies that tried and ran out of runway do not appear in the book.

For most seed and Series A companies the honest recommendation is **enter a category, win a segment, then re-frame from strength.** You earn the right to name a category by dominating something first. Figma did not create "collaborative design" as an unknown startup; it won browser-based design among specific teams and the category name followed.

If the user insists on category creation, the diagnostic questions are: how many quarters of runway can you spend on education before revenue, who else is investing in evangelising this frame with you (a category with one vendor is a product), and what is your Lightning Strike — the concentrated moment that introduces the category and you simultaneously, which is a *category* announcement rather than a product one.

## Messaging house

Once positioning is set, the messaging house makes it usable by other people.

```
                    ONE-LINE NARRATIVE
                the shift and the promised land
    ┌───────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┐
    │  PILLAR 1     │  PILLAR 2     │  PILLAR 3     │
    │  value theme  │  value theme  │  value theme  │
    ├───────────────┼───────────────┼───────────────┤
    │ proof         │ proof         │ proof         │
    │ metric        │ metric        │ metric        │
    │ customer      │ customer      │ customer      │
    │ quote         │ quote         │ quote         │
    └───────────────┴───────────────┴───────────────┘
              PROOF FLOOR: what makes it credible
        certifications · customers · data · guarantees
```

Rules: **three pillars, never five** — nobody remembers five. Each pillar is a *value theme*, not a feature. Each needs a metric and a real customer quote; a pillar without proof is a claim and will be discounted by buyers.

Per ICP tier, vary the *emphasis order* of the pillars, not the pillars themselves. Different pillars per segment means you have two products or no positioning.

## Positioning statement

```
For [ICP with trigger event]
who [job to be done, in their words]
[product] is the [market category — the frame where you obviously win]
that [single differentiated outcome, traceable to a unique attribute]
unlike [the true competitive alternative, usually "nothing" or a spreadsheet]
because [STRUCTURAL reason a funded incumbent cannot trivially copy this]
```

Audit only the last line. "Better UX," "more accurate," "purpose-built," "AI-native" are all copyable within two quarters of you proving demand. Structural reasons are: data that compounds with usage, a channel the incumbent cannot use without cannibalising itself, a business model that breaks their revenue recognition, a workflow they cannot adopt without a rewrite, a certification that took two years, or a schlep nobody well-funded wants to do.

If the "because" is empty, say the positioning is undefended and name what would fill it. Do not fill it with an adjective to be polite.
