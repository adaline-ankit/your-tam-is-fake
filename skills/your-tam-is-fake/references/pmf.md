# Product-market fit, measured

Load when the user claims PMF, denies PMF, or asks whether they should scale. **This is the most consequential diagnostic in the entire skill**, because scaling without PMF is the most expensive mistake available to a startup and it always looks like a GTM problem from the inside.

## Why it matters that PMF is measurable

The founder-facing version of PMF ("you'll know it when you feel it") is useless, because founders feel it constantly. The measurable versions are better because they can return "no."

The strategist's job: when someone says "our GTM isn't working," **first determine whether the GTM is the problem at all.** Weak retention, weak referral, and a chaotic ICP are PMF problems wearing GTM clothing, and no amount of channel work fixes them. Diagnosing a PMF problem as a GTM problem is how companies burn a Series A.

## The Sean Ellis 40% test

Ask users: *"How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?"* — very disappointed / somewhat disappointed / not disappointed.

**≥40% "very disappointed" is the PMF threshold**, benchmarked across roughly a hundred startups.

Rules for using it honestly:
- **Survey activated users**, not signups. Someone who never used the product cannot be disappointed to lose it.
- **n ≥ 40** for the number to mean anything; below that it is a vibe with a percent sign.
- **Segment the results.** This is the part everyone skips and the part that carries the value.

## The Superhuman engine — the important part

Rahul Vohra's contribution was turning a static score into an optimisation loop. First run: **22% very disappointed**, despite press coverage and a waitlist — the kind of external validation that convinces a team they have PMF when they do not. Within four months, **58%**, by doing this:

1. **Segment the "very disappointed" cohort.** Find who loves it. Superhuman's was speed-obsessed professionals processing 100+ emails a day.
2. **Rebuild around that segment**, explicitly at the cost of appeal to others.
3. **Split the rest.** Ignore the "not disappointed" group entirely — they will drag you toward the mean. Work only the *"somewhat disappointed"* group, and only where their blocker overlaps with what the loving segment also wants.
4. **Re-measure quarterly.** PMF is a rate, not a milestone, and it decays as you broaden.

The strategic lesson is not the survey. It is that **a mediocre overall score usually contains an excellent segment score**, and that segment is your beachhead. When a founder reports 25% overall, the right next question is: what is the score among the top decile of usage? If that segment is at 55%, they do not have a PMF problem, they have a targeting problem — a completely different, much cheaper fix.

## Retention curve flattening

The behavioural counterpart, and harder to fool than a survey.

Plot cohort retention by period since signup. Three shapes:

- **Flattens above zero** → PMF. A durable base exists; growth compounds on it. The height of the asymptote is roughly your ceiling.
- **Decays to zero** → no PMF, regardless of what the survey says. You are renting users.
- **Smiles** (declines then rises) → PMF in a subset, plus an onboarding or activation problem. Often the most encouraging shape, and frequently misread as failure.

Two rules: **cohort it, never blend it** — blended retention hides a collapsing new-cohort curve behind a loyal old base. And **use the natural usage frequency** as the period. Weekly for a daily-use tool, monthly for a workflow tool, quarterly for something touched at period close. Wrong period, meaningless curve.

## Other signals, ranked by how hard they are to fake

1. **Organic pull** — inbound you did not generate, users arriving via word of mouth, prospects already using a competitor's name for the category. Hardest to fake.
2. **Expansion without a rep** — accounts growing seats or usage with no sales involvement. Very hard to fake.
3. **Renewal at price**, no discount asked. Hard to fake.
4. **Usage depth** — the product used in the workflow it was bought for, at the frequency it was bought for.
5. **Sean Ellis score**, properly segmented.
6. **Sales-cycle compression** in a stable segment — the market is educating itself.
7. **NPS, logo counts, funding, press.** Easy to fake, easy to buy, and the four things most likely to appear on a slide claiming PMF.

## GTM fit is a separate gate

PMF and GTM fit are different, and conflating them is a common and expensive error.

- **PMF** — the people who use it will not give it up.
- **GTM fit** — you can find and acquire those people **repeatedly and profitably**, without a founder in the room.

You can have PMF and no GTM fit: a beloved product acquired entirely through the founder's network and heroics. That company has a real business and no scalable motion, and it will fail if it hires reps. You cannot have GTM fit without PMF — acquisition on a leaky product is just paying to churn faster.

The GTM-fit test is repeatability, and it is a process test, not a revenue test: *can you complete "I can sell this when I talk to [X] who has [Y]"?* If the last ten wins share no ICP and no trigger, there is no motion to scale yet, and hiring converts cash into nothing.

## Verdict language for this diagnosis

Be blunt, because the cost of hedging here is a wasted year:

- **No PMF** → "Do not spend on acquisition. Every dollar buys a customer you will lose. Fix retention first, and here is the cohort that suggests where fit already exists."
- **PMF in a segment, not overall** → "You have fit with [segment]. Your problem is targeting, not product. Narrow, dominate, then broaden." Usually the best news a founder can get and it rarely feels like it.
- **PMF, no GTM fit** → "The product works. The motion is a founder, and a founder is not a motion. Do not hire reps until you can complete the repeatability sentence."
- **Both** → "Scale it. Here is the capacity model and the constraint you will hit first."
