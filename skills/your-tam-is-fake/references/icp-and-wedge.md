# ICP and wedge

Load when the user describes their target customer in a sentence containing the word "and".

## The ICP test

An ICP is valid only if you can answer all six without hedging:

1. **Firmographics** — industry (a code, not a vibe), employee band, revenue band, geography.
2. **Technographics** — what is in their stack that your product needs or replaces.
3. **Trigger** — what happened recently that makes this urgent *this quarter*. New exec hire, funding round, audit finding, compliance date, migration, outage, layoff.
4. **Buyer** — the title that owns the budget. Not "leadership."
5. **Champion** — the title that feels the pain daily. Often not the buyer, which is the whole difficulty of enterprise sales.
6. **Count** — roughly how many exist, and the specific query that produces the list.

Fail any one and you have a market, not an ICP. Markets cannot be emailed.

## Why "SMB and enterprise" is fatal

They are different products wearing the same name. Different price points, different sales cycles, different security requirements, different support burden, different churn drivers, different onboarding, different word-of-mouth graph. Serving both from a seed-stage team means doing both at 40%.

The correct move is a **beachhead**: one segment narrow enough that you can become the obvious default inside it within 12 months, chosen because it has an adjacency you can expand into afterwards.

Crossing-the-chasm logic, still true, still ignored: you do not need a big first market. You need a **completely winnable** one. 60% of a segment beats 0.5% of a market, both in revenue and — decisively — in referenceability. Twenty customers who all know each other generate more pipeline than two hundred scattered ones.

## Choosing the wedge

The wedge is the *narrowest valuable thing* you can be undeniably best at. Score candidates:

| Criterion | Ask |
|-----------|-----|
| **Acute** | Is this a top-3 priority for the buyer *this quarter*, or a nice-to-have? |
| **Frequent** | Daily pain compounds into habit and retention. Annual pain gets a spreadsheet. |
| **Measurable** | Can the buyer prove the win in a number? Unprovable value dies at renewal. |
| **Unowned** | Is there a default answer today? If yes, you need 10x, not 2x. |
| **Expandable** | Does winning here earn the right to sell the next thing, or is it a dead end? |
| **Reachable** | Can you find and contact these people for less than the ACV justifies? |

A wedge that scores high on acute + frequent + measurable but low on expandable is a good business and a bad venture case. **Say which one you think it is** — founders often want the second while describing the first, and that mismatch is worth naming out loud.

## The real competitor

In most markets, the leading competitor is **nothing**: a spreadsheet, an intern, a Slack channel, an accepted cost of doing business. Nothing has a $0 price point, infinite incumbency, zero migration cost, and no procurement review. It wins most evaluations.

Beating nothing requires either a forcing function (compliance, an audit, a mandate, an outage) or a step-change in convenience large enough to overcome the fact that the status quo requires no decision. Ranking yourself against venture-funded competitors while ignoring nothing is the most common competitive-analysis error there is.

## Positioning that survives a bake-off

Fill in, and every blank must be falsifiable:

```
For [ICP with trigger]
who [job to be done]
[product] is a [category]
that [single differentiated outcome]
unlike [the actual default, which is usually "nothing"]
because [structural reason a well-funded incumbent cannot trivially copy this]
```

The last line is the one that matters and the one that is usually empty. "Better UX" is not a reason — UX is copyable in a quarter. Structural reasons: proprietary data that compounds with usage, a distribution channel the incumbent cannot use without cannibalising itself, a business model that breaks their revenue, a workflow they cannot adopt without a rewrite, a regulatory posture that took two years to earn.

If the differentiation is a feature, assume a well-capitalised incumbent ships it within two quarters of you proving the demand — and that they will announce it the week of your Series A.
