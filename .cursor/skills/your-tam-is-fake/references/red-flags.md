# Red flags — the catalogue

Load during the Attack phase. These are the patterns that predict failure. Finding one is not a kill; finding three that compound usually is.

## Market and sizing

1. **Top-down TAM with a 1% capture plan.** Covered exhaustively in `market-sizing.md`. Still the most common.
2. **The market exists but the budget does not.** Real problem, no line item, no owner. You are selling budget creation, which takes an extra quarter and a champion willing to spend political capital.
3. **A vitamin priced like a painkiller.** Nice-to-have products can be good businesses at nice-to-have prices. They cannot carry enterprise pricing and enterprise sales cycles.
4. **No "why now."** Every idea that works now failed before. If you cannot name the specific thing that changed — a cost curve crossing a threshold, a regulation with a date, a platform opening, a capability becoming reliable enough — then either the timing argument is missing or the idea is not new.
5. **Global TAM, single-market product.** One language, one currency, US-only compliance, then a worldwide market size.
6. **Counting the aware, not the urgent.** "Everyone has this problem" is usually true and usually irrelevant. Everyone has back pain; few people buy a mattress this month.

## ICP and demand

7. **"Everyone is our customer."** Also appears as "we're horizontal." A horizontal product still needs a vertical entry point.
8. **Enthusiasm from people who cannot buy.** Practitioners love it, procurement has never heard of it. Champion without a buyer is a demo, not a pipeline.
9. **Founder-network validation.** The first ten customers were friends, ex-colleagues, or investors' portfolio companies. Zero information about repeatability. Ask: how many came from a channel that could produce a hundred more?
10. **Pilots that never convert.** A stalled pilot is a "no" with better manners. Track pilot→paid conversion explicitly; three enthusiastic pilots and no contracts is a finding, not a pipeline.
11. **Interest with no behaviour change.** They said yes in the call and did not open the product. Stated preference is worthless; revealed preference is everything.

## Competition

12. **Ignoring "nothing."** The status quo is the market leader. See `icp-and-wedge.md`.
13. **A feature, not a product.** If the incumbent can ship this in a quarter and bundle it free, you have done their R&D. Ask what stops them, in structural terms.
14. **Competing on price against a balance sheet.** Whoever can lose money longest wins a price war. That is almost never the startup.
15. **The graveyard nobody checked.** Companies already tried this and died. Their post-mortems are public and specific. Not knowing why they died means repeating it with better branding.
16. **Differentiation that is a demo.** Impressive in a controlled 5-minute walkthrough, unremarkable on real data at real scale.

## Motion and economics

17. **ACV/motion mismatch.** See `gtm-motions.md`. Structurally fatal, frequently invisible until the cash runs out.
18. **CAC excluding salaries.** Understates by 2–4x.
19. **LTV from an unobserved churn rate.** A projection wearing a measurement's clothes.
20. **Scaling on broken retention.** NRR under 100% and the plan is to raise a round for paid acquisition. That converts cash into churn, efficiently.
21. **Only the founder can sell it.** No transferable qualification, no repeatable pitch, every deal a bespoke story.
22. **Unmodelled gross margin.** Especially AI inference cost. See `pricing-and-unit-economics.md`.
23. **Channel as a fix for weak demand.** Partners amplify fit, they do not create it.
24. **The hockey stick with no mechanism.** Flat, flat, flat, then up-and-to-the-right at month 9. What happens in month 9? If the answer is "the funding closes," the plan is a hope.

## Organisational and self-deception

25. **Metric that only goes up.** Cumulative signups, total users ever, "communities reached." Chosen because the honest metric is flat.
26. **A pivot narrated as a strategy.** Nothing wrong with pivoting; something wrong with pretending the new direction was always the plan, because then nobody learns why the old one failed.
27. **Roadmap as a substitute for positioning.** "We'll differentiate once we ship Q3." You differentiate now with what exists, or you do not sell now.
28. **Awards, logos, and lists standing in for revenue.** Being on a "top 50 startups" list is a marketing output, not a demand signal.
29. **Confusing fundraising with traction.** Investors are not customers. They are a different market with different criteria, and being good at one predicts little about the other.
30. **Hiring ahead of proof.** VP Sales before repeatability, demand-gen lead before a converting channel, partner manager before direct works.

## Compounding failure

Watch for these clusters. Individually survivable, jointly terminal:

- **The zombie:** real problem + no budget + long cycle + low ACV. Sells, slowly, forever, never at scale.
- **The feature:** thin differentiation + strong incumbent + horizontal ICP. Acquisition-at-best, and only if the incumbent bothers.
- **The treadmill:** low NRR + high CAC + paid acquisition dependence. Growth stops the day spend stops.
- **The mirage:** friends-and-family customers + founder-only selling + top-down TAM. Looks like traction, is not, and the discovery happens at the Series A.

## How to use this list

Do **not** dump it. Pick the two or three that are actually load-bearing for this specific plan, and for each one give:

- the specific evidence it applies here,
- what would have to be true for it not to matter,
- the cheapest test that resolves it this month,
- the cost of being wrong.

A list of thirty risks is not analysis, it is a way of appearing thorough while making no call. Rank, or you have done nothing.
